# Equal daylight, different animal time?
## Testing seasonal asymmetry in mammalian diel activity across landscapes

**Status:** Ecology manuscript v0 — Introduction, Methods, exploratory source-photo Results, independently reanalyzed ERA5 ecological context and Discussion; NO exposure-calibrated activity, causal mechanism or cross-region universality result  
**Date:** 2026-10-15  
**Companion evidence:** [ecological protocol](../docs/solar_seasonal_hysteresis_ecology_paper_protocol_20261015.md) | [machine-readable source gates](../research/solar_seasonal_hysteresis_source_feasibility_v0.json) | [issue #266](https://github.com/zuizui0223/odsp/issues/266).

### Intended conceptual advance

Many mammals reallocate activity within the 24-hour cycle across seasons. Sunrise and sunset define an obvious periodic forcing, but the rest of an organism's seasonal environment—temperature, vegetation, reproductive state, resource supply and human exposure—does not necessarily follow daylength instantaneously. The sun can therefore occupy nearly the same astronomical state on two separate dates while the terrestrial ecological conditions differ.

This suggests a natural counterfactual question: **If daylength is held almost constant in the same landscape, does a given species use the central daylight hours, dawn/dusk-adjacent daylight and night in the same proportions on the increasing and decreasing photoperiod branches?**

We refer to a branch contrast at matched photoperiod as **observational seasonal asymmetry** (or an observational loop). This wording deliberately does not presume that individual animals encode or remember the direction of the annual light cycle. A stronger physiological photoperiod hysteresis mechanism would require experimental or otherwise independent evidence.

### Closest ecological precedents and sharpened novelty (source checked 2026-10-15)

Seasonal switching between nocturnal, diurnal and crepuscular niches is **already established**. Continuous intraperitoneal activity meters on 12 free-roaming Arabian oryx from two desert sites found summer nocturnal/crepuscular versus winter diurnal allocation with strong temperature and photoperiod associations (2017, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.physbeh.2017.04.014). A Northern Ireland year-round camera-trap study (8,761 detections) already reported species-specific seasonal changes *relative to sunrise and sunset* across several mammal species (2018, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6252065/). Japanese Hokkaido mammal cameras reported seasonal diurnal peak changes in eight species (2016, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0163602), while tropical/subtropical Chinese muntjac and wild boar studies also report seasonal diel plasticity (2026, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gecco.2026.e04334).

Consequently this manuscript **must NOT claim novelty** from (1) day/night seasonal shifts, (2) correcting clocks to sunrise, (3) relating diel activity to temperature or photoperiod, or (4) seasonal mammal temporal niche plasticity itself. The testable addition, if source coverage permits, is **same-photoperiod within-site opposite-branch comparisons**, the *orthogonal* daylight-core-to-edge versus whole-daylight-to-night response signatures, and transfer of *environmentally conditioned response differences* to wholly new landscapes with distinct sensor/source uncertainties. One tropical landscape alone cannot establish this broad claim.

### Introduction (working draft)

Sunrise and sunset organize animal activity across the world, but time-of-day niches are not mechanically fixed to solar geometry. Species differ in whether they are active during the day, night or twilight, and changes in these distributions can alter encounter rates, resource access and patterns of potential interaction. Importantly, the observation of temporal shifts does not on its own identify competition: weather, food supply, habitat selection and detection processes also affect when wildlife cameras record animals.

Classical day-night classification poses a second problem. The astronomical duration of daylight varies across seasons and latitudes. A species with invariant behavior relative to the sun can appear to change its clock-time distribution, particularly near a fixed clock-bin boundary. Solar-time standardization partly addresses this geometrical discrepancy, but the comparison between one solar-transformed model and one direct civil-clock model is not necessarily fair if their attainable probability distributions differ. Solar phase is a useful *coordinate system*, not itself evidence of a causal zeitgeber.

Existing ecological evidence already documents strong seasonal differences in camera-trap encounter rates and in diel activity. For instance, Ikeda and colleagues (2016) described season-dependent activity peaks among mammals in Hokkaido, whereas Kays and colleagues (2020) found seasonality in occupancy or detection rates in temperate and tropical study sites. These studies provide a biological starting point but did not establish a cross-landscape law of within-species diel redistribution **at fixed photoperiod on both seasonal branches**.

We hypothesize that observed diel allocation contains information about seasonal ecological context beyond instantaneous astronomical daylength. We distinguish two biologically different possible responses: (i) a shift from midday-centered to dawn/dusk-adjacent daylight use, consistent with—but not proving—daylight thermal refuge, and (ii) a shift from all daylight to the night, potentially reflecting broader heat, resource or disturbance constraints. Different taxa need not respond in the same direction; the more general question is whether response heterogeneity can be predicted from environmental differences at matched daylength.

Testing this hypothesis requires a stricter unit of ecological replication than photographs. Events must be dated and linked to an identified physical site whose real camera operation is known at the relevant time of day, and rising and falling branch observations must be available without post hoc selection on sightings. Variation across cameras in one study region tests local reproducibility; independently sampled regions are necessary to test ecological generality.

We therefore separate source-admissibility checks from a predeclared biological analysis, and require new-region predictions for the strongest conclusions. Failure to acquire adequate simultaneous source effort is an identification limit rather than an ecological negative result.

### Predictions to test

**P1 (light-only null):** same physical site × same species × matched daylength implies no rising/falling branch contrast in the relative detected daylight-core, daylight-edge or night intensities after real operational effort adjustment.

**P2 (core-to-edge):** greater falling-branch heat exposure relative to rising dates predicts a positive edge/core seasonal contrast. Lower exposure, winter or differently timed rainfall may produce a null or negative effect.

**P3 (daylight-to-night):** night/daylight redistribution is partially distinct from P2. A species can use dawn/dusk-adjacent *daylight* more without increasing night use.

**P4 (ecological transfer):** environmental and guild variables predict the direction and magnitude of branch contrasts in an independent region held out before model selection. A pooled mean effect with strongly inconsistent regions is insufficient evidence of universality.

### Methods (registered intent; source admission pending)

**Unit of observation.** Event species, dated local time, physical station identity, solar boundaries and original independently sourced operating intervals. Report camera detection rather than unmeasured latent movement or activity. Explicitly check local time zone, daylight-saving changes and long nights crossing midnight. For multi-year designs match each year separately and preserve dates with verified camera uptime and zero records.

**Astronomical matcher.** Pair increasing and decreasing photoperiod dates by site-specific astronomical daylength only, before analyzing observed wildlife events. If original site/date pair freeze exists (Uljin, 41 pairs), use that frozen roster unchanged. Retain the absolute daylength mismatch as an audit variable and independently handle equation-of-time changes in civil solar noon.

**Time categories.** Central 50% of daylight = core; combined first/last 25% of daylight = edge; all outside sunrise/sunset = night. Compute zone operating-hours from hardware/deployment records with any verified downtime. Do not mistake the published Korean civil twilight period outside daylight for our daylight edge.

**Biological endpoints.** Estimate two separate site/species seasonal log-ratios, H_edge and H_night, through an exposure-offset count model with explicitly preselected pooling and zero-count rules. Cluster uncertainty at physical sites and independent landscapes. Treat the nested model's comparison as a conditional observational prediction, not evidence of intrinsic circadian memory.

**Environmental explanation.** On the same matched dates compute daily temperature, thermal exposure during daylight core, precipitation, vegetation greenness and their branch differences from separately sourced, versioned climate and remote-sensing products. Use environmental predictors as observational explanations and test lag associations, not as natural experiments that uniquely identify temperature causation.

**Cross-landscape replication.** Train in independently sampled study regions, then hold out a complete region and assess the predictive distribution of the observed log-ratios. Report a new-region prediction interval, heterogeneity and direction as well as average effects. Within-camera sites are clustered, not independent landscape replications.

**Sensitivity and falsification.** Stable-solar-phase source-free counterexample, station-composition shifts, seasonal camera detection probability q, timestamp uncertainties, partial on-hours, known alternative calendar windows, external species taxon variation and negative controls. Favor the source-level HOLD over a spurious precise confidence interval.

### Results — exploratory original-source **photograph time**, not animal activity

Full source and numerical reproducibility: [2026-10-15 two-study ecological results](../docs/solar_seasonal_real_exploratory_results_20261015.md). These post-publication observational comparisons were separately frozen relative to their newly generated outcome tables; they do not establish independent preregistered confirmation of a biological phenomenon.

**East African Serengeti**. In the independently published Snapshot Serengeti original observation archive, 334,671 source consensus rows and 225 original site ID values were verified against original checksums. Original source date-only checking found 186 sites with at least 20 candidate opposite-side December-solstice dates, giving 25,016 prospective **date** pairs (not source-confirmed active hours). After original source public-coordinate sunrise/sunset calculation and ≤1-minute matched-daylength restriction, 24,290 station-date pairs remained across 186 source sites, with 38,792 author-source recorded and quality-filtered detections on accepted dates. Among the original photo-recorded source labels, 43 taxa had observations, and the frozen rule nominated 15 labels for readable descriptive comparison (one nonmammal, guinea fowl, retained by original all-taxa rule, not asserted a mammal).

The falling-versus-rising branch camera-site×solstice-cycle stratified **recorded event odds ratios** differed by species and response axis. For spotted hyena, daylight edge/core 1.34 and night/daylight 2.63; for female lion, 2.63 and 2.74; for zebra, 1.08 and 1.41; for buffalo, 0.70 and 1.36; and for Thomson's gazelle, 0.62 and 0.79. This is an interesting example of observed species-specific reallocations rather than a common directional effect, but the data **do not** identify latent behavioral activity because true site×date×hour ON exposure and `q` are unknown.

**Independent Northern Ireland published camera source**. Caravaggi et al.'s tagged v1.2.1 source contains **9,411** raw rows; applying the original authors' four named exclusion groups exactly yields the published **8,761** independent detections. Original `all_data.csv` has local sunrise/sunset and dates (plus 25 anomalous 2001-year rows excluded from the present 2013–2016 dating comparison, not corrected). Only 13 site-years contain photographing dates on both annual sides of June 21 with documented sunrise/sunset daylight difference ≤10 min, yielding 353 **record-detected date-pairs**, 988 matched-period source photo records. One site-year contributes 51.56% of those detected-day match candidates. Literal taxon names contain case/trailing-space duplicates; original exploratory outcome preserves all labels, including zero and undefined values. For literal source `Badger` (507 events), recorded edge/core MH odds 0.50 and night/daylight 0.20; for `Fox` (63), 2.80 and 1.98. The matched date selection is CONDITIONED on detection presence (there are no independent original ON-day logs), so this does NOT independently verify biological seasonality, despite the geographically distinct landscape and author source authentication.

**Independent ERA5 environmental context, replayed against the ORIGINAL source photo-date roster.** External public reanalysis for one broad Serengeti representative climatic grid, extracted from Open-Meteo ERA5 without sampling original station GPS, covered the original 2010–2012 December-solstice cycles. An initial frozen source-agnostic screen of **522** solstice-symmetric regional calendar dates gave mean falling-minus-rising daily air temperature maximum **−0.285°C** and mean preceding-30-day rainfall **+58.432 mm**. Its three annual temperature-maximum means had opposite signs, +0.398, −0.763 and −0.491°C. This independently demonstrates that equal sunlight geometry need not imply warmer ecological conditions on the declining-photoperiod branch.

A stricter separately frozen **post-existing-outcomes** source replay aligned the exact original 24,290 sun-matched **physical-site×date** pair support to that same region-level ERA5 grid. Source support comprised **4,718/9,769/9,803** date pairs across 2010/2011/2012, from **108/135/130** original station IDs. Corresponding descriptive site/date-weighted weather differences were falling-minus-rising daily maximum temperature **+0.160/−0.060/−0.566°C** and antecedent 30-day precipitation **+61.158/+11.848/+48.751 mm**, respectively. The original pooled 43-taxon receipt, 38,792 PHOTO-records, original event/effort MD5s and ERA5 daily-array SHA256 were all independently reproduced exactly before these quantities were opened ([source-matched year-cycle result](../docs/serengeti_actual_solar_photo_era5_cycle_joint_result_20261015.md), CI [38700484637](https://github.com/zuizui0223/odsp/actions/runs/38700484637)). These are **not 24,290 independent climate replicates**; there is one broad ERA5 grid and three annual cycles.

Within the same unmodified source-record dataset, spotted hyena site-stratified night/day falling-vs-rising PHOTO odds were **3.367, 2.498 and 1.380** in the three cycles, and zebra were **2.017, 1.249 and 1.505**. Both taxa retained a nightward photo-record signal in cycles with **negative or approximately zero average regional falling-minus-rising daytime maximum temperature**, weakening a simplistic explanation that all apparent photo-time changes are responses to hotter declining-daylight seasons. Species and within-day edge/daylight/night axes were not uniformly aligned; for example, buffalo night/day recorded odds were **0.848, 1.468, 1.444**, while its within-day edge/core odds remained **0.554, 0.777, 0.694**. There were no independent detector-q, camera on-hour, animal movement, vegetation-NDVI or forage time series. These are observational photo-time differences, **not proof of cooling-induced night behavior, hydroclimatic causation or predator–prey response**.

**Current ecological interpretation.** This creates a cross-continent **descriptive observation** that relative detected photograph-time distributions may change between similar-daylength solar dates, with nonidentical response directions among source taxa. It cannot quantify a universal population effect, determine thermal or phenological causes, or claim valid external predictive generalization. Different algorithms selected solstice mirrored dates (Serengeti) and available photograph-detected similar daylight dates (Ireland), and both sources lack independently measured time-specific camera ON-hours and calibration of `q`. A new data source with actual event-independent station-hour opportunity evidence and an independent region is needed for the original manuscript's intended H1–H4 tests.

**Proposed full manuscript figures, conditional on new qualified data:** (1) original rising/falling dates and verified environmental lag; (2) all eligible species × region H_edge and H_night with site-clustered uncertainty; (3) independently withheld regional prediction/calibration; (4) q/effort and geometrical sensitivity. None is a currently justified causal or meta-analytic result.

In the largest predator–potential-prey comparison, hyena versus zebra, equal physical camera seasonal difference was near zero: 26 of 58 cameras positive, 30 negative and 2 tied. The camera-year-equal sign and magnitude changed across annual cycles (2010 −18.39 pp at 19 site-years, 2011 +0.50 pp at 37, 2012 +7.06 pp at 18). This does **not** establish a common directional predator-greater-night-shift at overlapping camera stations, even though the distinct *full-sample species-specific night/day odds* were hyena ~2.63 and zebra ~1.41. These quantities differ in scale, samples and weighting; neither an arithmetic subtraction of odds nor a Simpson-type causal decomposition is justified.

The apparent female-lion–wildebeest difference was based on only 10 cameras, with zero eligible paired stations in the 2010 solstice cycle. It was not possible to calculate the stricter full eight-cell relative three-way log odds at ANY eligible lion–wildebeest source station-year without fabricating pseudocounts. Similarly only 5/74 hyena–zebra site-years and 18/92 buffalo–zebra site-years had all eight observed source cells. Hence an assumption-dependent cancellation of common time-specific camera detection cannot credibly be generalized to the co-recorded station network.

This **new post-outcome exploratory source result** is a useful ecological limit: season-dependent timing of species' observed photo records does **not** by itself imply season-dependent *relative timing between candidate predators and prey at sites where both are photographed*. It is not a null hypothesis acceptance, a test of real animal predation, a latent activity conclusion or an independent spatial sampling p-value. ### Added Results — the apparent annual PHOTO-time shift has two observational components

A separately frozen post-first-outcome source audit decomposed species-level changes in NIGHT photograph fractions, under common camera support, into (i) **within-camera** change in the time distribution of PHOTO records, and (ii) **among-camera** reweighting of the locations contributing those same source records. This distinction matters in the spatially heterogeneous Serengeti, where previous work documents seasonal herbivore migrations and rainfall/productivity gradients. The decomposition itself is a standard exact arithmetic identity; it is not evidence of species migration or a new ecological theorem.

For species `s`, solstice-year `y` and source camera `i` with at least one source PHOTO in *both* seasonal branches, denote the NIGHT photo share in each branch by `p_ib`, and the branch-specific fraction of original photos contributed by that same camera by `w_ib`. Then the common-camera NIGHT source-photo difference is exactly:

`P_F−P_R = Σ_i [0.5(w_iF+w_iR)(p_iF−p_iR)] + Σ_i [0.5(p_iF+p_iR)(w_iF−w_iR)]`

The first is the *within-camera photographed-time term W* and the second is *between-camera photographed-location-weight term B*. This identity is defined only on cameras whose species-photo records are present in BOTH branches. Source all-camera photo-night differences need not equal `W+B`, because source cameras with only one branch of photographs are outside this common roster. They are retained in source eligibility and missing-support audits, **not assigned pseudo activity fractions**.

The original `38,792` source photo detections, `24,290` original astronomy-matched station×date pairs, `186` cameras and all `43` source taxonomy labels were independently replayed and checked against the unchanged FIRST output (checksum required). All SIX new known-truth tests passed [CI 38738580588](https://github.com/zuizui0223/odsp/actions/runs/38738580588). All source taxon×year×branch×photographic daypart totals were reproduced before opening NEW decomposed results.

Among **129** source taxon-label×3-year entries, **24** entries from **10** source labels (including `guineaFowl`, a bird) met the predeclared descriptive camera support and coverage gates. In **17/24**, `|W|>|B|`; **7/24** had `|B|>|W|`, while **10/24** had *oppositely signed* W and B. These are descriptive comparisons from ONE ecosystem, not independent cross-taxon validation or an inferential test.

A striking difference appeared between **zebra** and **buffalo**, measured in NIGHT photograph percentage-point contribution at nearly equal source-daylength:

| Original taxon/year | Cameras common to both branch photo records | W within-camera PHOTO distribution | B between-camera PHOTO-location composition | Exact common-camera PHOTO night Δ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Zebra 2010 | 54 | +13.27 | −5.32 | +7.95 |
| Zebra 2011 | 102 | +6.10 | +0.69 | +6.79 |
| Zebra 2012 | 77 | +6.50 | +1.16 | +7.67 |
| Buffalo 2010 | 20 | +2.96 | −19.37 | −16.41 |
| Buffalo 2011 | 48 | +7.30 | −9.16 | −1.86 |
| Buffalo 2012 | 50 | +5.41 | +7.11 | +12.52 |

Zebra's W was positive in each of the three original annual cycles, including 2010 when the photo-location-mix term partially offset it. Its common photographed-camera roster included 73.7–98.3% of original rising/falling camera record totals in the respective year cycles. Buffalo's apparent 2011 photo-night stability partly resulted from **cancellation** of W (+7.30 pp) by B (−9.16 pp). The 2010 buffalo result **fails the original display-support rule** because only 41.3% of falling-branch records are represented in the common photo-positive camera roster, so it is shown for complete auditing, not treated as well-supported replication.

The point is not that zebra necessarily changed true activity while buffalo migrated. **Both processes are observational source photograph bookkeeping:** station-mixture B can also arise from unmeasured date/season/position/camera-hour detection differences, and W can arise from detection sensitivity changing within a station. Common-photo station support is a detection-conditioned selected subset. The within-versus-location distinction is a necessary condition for more meaningful ecological follow-up, not a substitute for independent animal activity measurements.

All 43 taxonomy labels, including low/undefined support, are in the machine-readable first receipt. ### Added Results — independent satellite greenness at the original PHOTO camera stations

We extended the source-preserving ecological analysis to ORIGINAL NASA/Terra MODIS `MOD13Q1` V061 vegetation reflectance, 250-m 16-day NDVI, rather than inferring vegetation from the atmospheric ERA5 rainfall series alone. Following a separately frozen satellite-source procedure, original source COG images were retrieved through Microsoft Planetary Computer public temporary SAS access and subjected to paired MODIS reliability QA=0 (good) and 3×3 250-m window assessment; at least six original good pixels were necessary for each observation, and only the last **fully completed** 16-day composite before each source calendar day (≤20 days old) was admitted. This is a remote-sensing environmental response, not a source-calibrated animal encounter rate.

The first source point test reproduced original NDVI **0.5484** with 9/9 best-quality pixels in the January 17–February 1, 2012 Terra composite. A full original 83-scene access route found **75** good regional source composites, qualifying **435/522** prior fixed same-daylength December21 ±k calendar pairs, with all three 2010–2012 annual-cycle mean falling-minus-rising NDVI values positive (**+0.145, +0.261, +0.168**). These regional environmental results alone do not identify resources at the physical camera sites.

A subsequent *post-prior-animal and post-regional-NDVI-outcome exploratory*, independently frozen source join linked all original **186** camera positions internally to the complete unchanged **83 original Terra satellite composites** and the exact first original animal result (source total **38,792** photo records, **24,290** photoperiod-caliper camera/date pairs, **43** source taxon labels). QA-qualified source site/day-pairs were **4,125/4,718** (2010), **7,476/9,769** (2011), and **8,177/9,803** (2012). Site/calendar-cycle NDVI difference estimates were reported only where at least **10** original paired dates and ≥60% original date-pair coverage yielded two good NDVI readings. This retained **98, 112 and 112** source camera-year units. **Every one of these 322 quality-qualified camera-year NDVI differences was positive**, and across-site mean falling-minus-rising NDVI was **+0.1646, +0.2727 and +0.2224** by 2010–2012 cycle, respectively.

To test whether stronger local greening was associated with greater PHOTO recording at that same physical camera, we restricted each focal taxon×year to camera sites with at least five original photo records in EACH seasonal branch on the NDVI-quality-matched photo dates, at least 15 qualified sites total, and ≥50% original full-photo data coverage on both branches; never smoothed zeros or optimized thresholds after seeing species effects. Only **three of the fifteen** previously disclosed five-taxon×three-cycle associations met these conditions: zebra 2011 (53 sites), zebra 2012 (31), and wildebeest 2011 (27). The Spearman rank correlations of camera-specific NDVI difference with log(falling/rising source photo count) were **+0.114, −0.049 and −0.0015**, respectively. The corresponding correlations with change in within-camera NIGHT PHOTO fraction were **+0.129, +0.312 and −0.071**. The other **12** low-support taxon-year cells were explicitly **UNAVAILABLE** for correlation and were not rescued by reducing n or adding photo pseudo-counts. Leave-one-camera values are descriptive sensitivity ranges, not animal-population confidence intervals, and no p-values were fitted.

Thus original camera-local *satellite-recorded vegetation greenness generally increased* on the declining-daylight side of near-matched photoperiod, but **the magnitude of greening did not show a consistent monotonic association with the camera's reweighting of photographed events**. The weak-to-moderate heterogeneity in within-camera PHOTO night-fraction correlations is hypothesis-generating, not proof that local green forage causes animal chronotype change. Satellites see foliage and canopy, not necessarily biomass/nitrogen of edible grass; original camera placement/vegetation trimming, optical flash hardware, seasonal q, actual camera ON-hours and off-grid animal movements remain confounded.

**No camera positions or original observation timestamps were exported.** The complete source, contract, quality/zero handling and limits are in [camera-site MODIS ecological report](../docs/serengeti_original_camera_modis_ndvi_photo_link_20261015.md) with original first source-locked [CI 38744196749](https://github.com/zuizui0223/odsp/actions/runs/38744196749); precursor regional complete satellite [CI 38742381644](https://github.com/zuizui0223/odsp/actions/runs/38742381644). This remains one ecosystem, no independently validated true animal activity or causal environmental response.

### Discussion — biological interpretation and what would falsify it

A species-general, monotonically positive effect of warmer *declining-daylight* weather on nightward activity is not supported by this original-source photographic evidence. Reanalysis of a source-independent regional ERA5 climate grid showed mean **cooling** in two of three annual cycles, while source photo-record odds for spotted hyena and zebra nevertheless pointed nightward within same-camera strata. This does not rule out fine-scale thermal regulation: local camera-hour temperatures, species physiology, individual sex/age, microhabitat, weather-dependent animal distance, camera sensitivity and seasonal detections can differ substantially from a landscape grid's daily 2-m T_max. No species causal-response coefficient is estimated.

The additional same-camera trophic comparison also places a strong restriction on the proposed biological story. Source-specific nightward shifts of two species do **not** produce a stable *relative* predator–prey seasonal shift at common camera stations in the best-supported hyena–zebra pair. Whether potential interactions become more or less synchronized therefore remains unproven; spatial co-detection, individual passage and event-time covariates are necessary for any ecological coupling interpretation.\n\nThe original source-photo decomposition has now demonstrated that **observed spatial-camera reweighting and same-camera photo-time composition can act in opposite directions**, but cannot distinguish movement from detection bias. A more biologically productive next hypothesis is *resource- and movement-conditioned temporal niche reorganization*: the state of the landscape depends on rainfall history and phenology as well as the sun. The Serengeti is especially compelling because herbivore migration and grazing dynamics follow rains, which can change both which animals a camera sees and when predators encounter available prey. Independent MODIS vegetation greenness is now measured at the original source camera pixels and rises in every quality-qualified site-year, but simple camera-by-camera greenness-change magnitude is not consistently correlated with the photographed record-weight response. Direct edible forage and grass nutrient availability, repeated actual physical camera ON-hours and independent animal encounter/detector-q calibration remain indispensable before distinguishing resource-driven movement from composition or optical-detection artefacts. Three weather cycles are inadequate to infer a generalized hydroclimate response, and the candidate Northern Ireland external source lacks independent observation support to confirm any analogous latent response.

A high-impact paper would use this source audit only as **hypothesis-generating ecological motivation** and test three competing models on untouched, independently sampled landscapes: (i) light-only phase invariance conditional on daylength, (ii) microclimate thermal displacement from daylight core into edge/night, and (iii) rainfall/vegetation-resource-associated temporal niche reassembly with species-specific guild consequences. The target is an independently held-out *environment-to-response prediction*, not a score competition between geometrically unequal civil and solar histogram models. Null, opposite-sign and source-unavailable results must remain part of that evaluation.

### What conclusions will be defensible?

- With matched date/site independent camera-hours but unknown q: differences in **detected encounter-time allocation** conditional on measurement constraints.
- With q reference that transports across stations and seasons: a better-constrained inference about actual activity timing; still not automatically causal.
- With multiple independently held-out regions and environment checks: portable predictive ecology, potentially a limited generality claim.
- With aggregate seasons, unverified site/date camera-hours or only falling-season recordings: descriptive/background evidence, NOT a direct positive or negative H test.
